## 1. Can you describe your experience as a Product Designer working directly with Product Managers and Engineers on complex digital products? What types of products or workflows have you designed?

At [Lumanu](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu) I lead product design, code much of the app UI myself, and work with the exec team on what we build, then lead design on tax, vendor compliance, and payments from inside product and engineering. Lumanu is the fintech master vendor for marketing partnerships and productions, and global brands use it to onboard, pay, and account for creators, freelancers, and agencies, so most of what I design is multi actor and rule driven. I designed a [tax engine](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#tax-engine) to automate taxes, research driven and shaped with users. I designed the [Projects](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#projects) feature for real time visibility, control, and cross team collaboration, and ran multiple rounds of research interviews across agencies, SMBs, enterprise, and DTC. I drove [multiple redesigns of vendor onboarding](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#vendor-onboarding-and-compliance) using Heap funnel analytics and CS ticket data, balancing compliance with user feedback. User feedback and CS tickets pointed to a missing [reporting feature](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#reporting-feature), so I built and launched it myself, and I designed, built, and animated a [role aware homepage](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#buyer-dashboard) that surfaces value and gives a clear, actionable overview. I also simplified [linking a withdrawal method](https://amirbeck.com/playground#withdrawal), starting with country and currency, then showing the withdrawal methods that work for that pair. Before Lumanu, at [SkySlope](https://amirbeck.com/skyslope), I improved the UX across [DigiSign](https://amirbeck.com/skyslope#digisign), [Forms](https://amirbeck.com/skyslope#forms), and [Breeze](https://amirbeck.com/skyslope#breeze), and led Breeze from concept through launch. At [Granate](https://amirbeck.com/granate) I led product design for an app that supports people through loss and end of life planning, and my work spanned UX, UI, branding, and product strategy. The constant is working close to a PM and to developers. On [EasyPeady](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady) the team was a PM, a UX researcher, a UX/UI designer, and six developers. I co-wrote a PRD with the PM to capture the problem statements and our MVP user flow, then we went to test if the tool had business viability, and [I ran a brainstorm with product and engineering](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#flows) where developers scored each solution's build complexity 1 to 5 while designers scored its UX, so we chose flows that were cheapest to build with the best UX.

## 2. Tell us about your experience working with established design systems in Figma. How have you used components, tokens, auto-layout, and existing patterns while also identifying when new patterns were needed?

At Lumanu [the design system and the codebase had drifted apart](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#design-system-and-ai-tooling), so I aligned them in component UI, naming, and functionality, then used Figma Code Connect for a 1 to 1 match between the system and the codebase, and now developers pick the right component easily, mockups match development 1 to 1, build time is faster with no manual mapping, and AI IDEs and code assistants work more accurately. Day to day that means building from what is already there. Reusable components and variants, tokens in Token Studio so a change moves through the system instead of being repainted screen by screen, and auto layout so a component behaves like the flex and grid it will become in code. I have also built systems from nothing. At [Granate](https://amirbeck.com/granate#design-system) I rebuilt every component under the new brand identity with an emphasis on usability, scalability, and team accessibility, and used dedicated Figma pages per component type for easy internal discovery and access, and because design work often got deprioritized with a small dev team, I split the redesign into four phases so we could ship it piece by piece, capture value in each release, without the app feeling inconsistent along the way. On [EasyPeady](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#the-pivot), with a quarter to launch, I compressed that into branding and every key component on one sheet, which allowed development to quickly spin up without blockers. I reach for the existing pattern first, and the bar for a new one is evidence. Our [DigiSign](https://amirbeck.com/skyslope#digisign) tool had an interaction pattern deviation from most other e-signing tools where the signature block stayed selected after placement, and usability testing found 4 of 10 testers were frustrated placing and assigning blocks and 5 of 10 struggled to change the properties of a block after placement. Solving this was not simple, since many long time users were used to the old interaction. The survey split was even, so I built two things, a preferences page to choose the selection behavior, and a clearer flow for changing signers that cut effort and confusion, and we saw a 50% reduction in digital signing mistakes. The other case was [permissions](https://amirbeck.com/playground#permissions) at Lumanu, where our roles could not meet the different requirements from buyer auditors, which is a data model problem that no rearrangement of the existing pattern fixes, so we moved to permission groups, where access can be set per user for enterprise companies with stricter compliance requirements.

## 3. Can you share an example of a project where you worked closely with engineers from design handoff through development? How did you manage technical tradeoffs and ensure the final shipped experience matched the intended design?

[EasyPeady](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady) is the clearest one. COVID required the PEAD-V form for every home viewing, introducing significant work for real estate agents, and given the nature of the timeliness of the issue we needed an MVP by the end of Q1 2021, ideally sooner, with a PM, a UX researcher, me, and six developers. The real problem was routing rather than screens, so [I mapped who acts at each step and when each email fires](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#flows), mapped every notification the flow depends on and handed it to the team, and laid out four ways to split the completed envelope. Every tradeoff got settled with engineering rather than handed to them. Research showed buyer agents were 46% more likely than seller agents to report difficulty, so the call was to scope the MVP to buyer agents, and what it cost was that seller agent use cases stayed out of the MVP. The PM and I then settled on splitting the completed envelope in the backend, because it removed the PDF split agents were doing manually, with a fallback we had to support just in case if we encountered technical constraints. To choose between options at all, I ran a brainstorm where developers scored each solution's build complexity 1 to 5 while designers scored its UX, so build cost and experience sat side by side. Then, [mid-development, SkySlope shifted strategy](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#the-pivot) from this tool being a paid feature inside our existing software, Forms, to a free standalone app, which meant that instead of just authenticating users with existing accounts we had to also support net new users. We rebranded mid-development because the development team was already ramped up, so I partnered with the visual designer on new branding, redesigned the key screens and created a basic style guide, which allowed development to quickly spin up without blockers, and I designed the UI alongside development, iterating as we built and clearing UX blockers via drop-in calls while trying to get ahead. [SkySlope's other products saw a 15% increase in usage](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#launch), the app generated a pipeline of new customers, and it became the groundwork for a future fully fledged product called Breeze, though [the fast schedule led to one high impact oversight](https://amirbeck.com/easypeady#retrospective), an authentication problem that surfaced only in user testing after the pivot, and more thorough testing would have helped us discover it before launch. The way I hold fidelity now is to remove the handoff altogether, which is why at Lumanu I coded [~90% of the app redesign](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#app-redesign) myself, with help on the left nav and complex layout files.

## 4. What experience do you have using AI-assisted design or development tools such as Claude and Cursor in your product design workflow? Please share a specific example of how you've used them alongside Figma.

I build and prototype with AI native, design first tools daily, including Cursor, Claude Code, Claude, Figma Make, Figma MCP, Code Connect, Paper, Framer AI, Motion, and the Vercel AI SDK, and what I have found is that they are only as good as the system you point them at, because a model reading a messy Figma file writes a messy approximation of your components while a model reading a mapped one writes your components. The specific example is [reporting at Lumanu](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#reporting-feature). User feedback and CS tickets pointed to a missing reporting feature, so I designed it in Figma on our existing system and built and launched it myself, and Figma Code Connect, the Figma MCP, and Claude Code took me from design to shipped code. None of that works until the system is accurate, so the prerequisite was [aligning the design system and the codebase](https://amirbeck.com/lumanu#design-system-and-ai-tooling) in component UI, naming, and functionality and wiring them together with Code Connect. With that in place the Figma MCP lets Claude Code read the actual frame, its structure, tokens, spacing, and component instances, instead of me describing it or pasting a screenshot, and because the mapping exists what comes back uses our components and our tokens rather than a lookalike built out of divs, which is the usual failure mode and the thing that makes AI generated UI expensive to clean up. I still review the output as a designer, for token misuse, spacing that drifted, states nobody thought about, and keyboard and focus behavior. The result was a new reporting feature which allowed our clients to audit their finances. I work the same way on [this site](https://amirbeck.com), which was a Framer build I rebuilt by hand in Next.js. The layout, type scale, and palette all started in Figma, components map to their Figma source so the design system and the codebase share one vocabulary of component names, props, and structure, and I prototyped the motion timing with AI tools, then held it to a high bar. AI takes the translation work out of design to code, but it does not decide what is worth building, and it did not catch what Framer had quietly broken, including illustrations cropped by their own frames, images served far larger than they render, and text and tap targets under accessible minimums.
